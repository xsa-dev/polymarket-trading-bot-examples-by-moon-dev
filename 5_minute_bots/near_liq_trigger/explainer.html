Slide 1 / 5The thing that starts it all

A liquidation is a forced sale.

Someone borrowed money to bet big. They ran out of room. Now the exchange sells for them — whether they like it or not.

A trader puts in $2,500 of his own money and borrows enough to control $100,000 of Bitcoin. That is called 40x leverage.

Here is the catch. If Bitcoin drops about 2.5%, his $2,500 is gone. So the exchange steps in and sells everything for him. That is a liquidation.

The trader in this picture already lost most of his cushion. He is now only 0.14% away from the line. That is about $92 of Bitcoin movement.

And this is the part that matters to us: a forced sale is a real order hitting the market. Forced selling pushes the price down. Forced buying pushes it up.

Slide 2 / 5Why a 5-minute market hits so hard

A $5 bet that punches like $1,000.

Bitcoin barely moves in five minutes. On a 5-minute market, barely moving is enough to double your money — or take all of it.

In a normal 5 minutes, Bitcoin moves about 0.1%. That is almost nothing.

The whale at 40x? That 0.1% move changes his money by 4%.

You on a 5-minute market? You buy “Down” for 50¢. If you are right, it pays $1.00. That same tiny move just gave you +100%.

100% divided by 0.1% is about 1,000x. Your $5 hits like $1,000 of Bitcoin.

And there are 288 of these markets every single day — one every five minutes, all day and all night.

The good news: you can never lose more than the $5 you put in. Nobody can liquidate you. The whale can lose everything he has.

Slide 3 / 5Step one — find the ledge

Right now, all the fuel is below us.

Longs get sold off below the price. Shorts get bought back above it. So look at both sides and ask: which pile is closer?

This is a real photo of the market at 11:59 AM ET. Every bar is one big trader, parked at the exact price where they get force-sold.

Inside half a percent below us: nine big traders holding $9.98 million.

Inside half a percent above us: nobody. The nearest short is more than twice as far away.

So the market is tilted. If price slips a little, it falls into a wall of forced selling — and that selling pushes it down even further, into the next guy.

Our bot now points DOWN. But it does not buy yet.

Slide 4 / 5Step two — wait for the first domino

Being close is not a reason to bet.

The bot sits still until one real person actually gets wiped out. That first forced sale is the proof the fall has started.

A trader can hover near his line for hours and never break. So “close” is not enough.

The bot waits for a real liquidation of $5,000 or more, on the same side, in the last two minutes.

That is the first domino. Someone smaller already got knocked over, and he is falling toward the big pile.

The second it prints, the bot buys and then does nothing else. No selling, no panic, no changing its mind. It holds until the five minutes are up.

Slide 5 / 5The honest part

Here is how this loses money.

The price of a share is really the crowd's guess at the odds. To win, we have to be a better guesser than the whole crowd.

When we pay 21¢, the market is telling us it thinks there is a 21% chance of Down.

So we must be right more than 21% of the time just to break even. Being right 21% of the time earns exactly $0.

Losing hurts in a way that is easy to miss: every loss is a total loss of that $5. There is no “down a little.” It is all or nothing, up to 288 times a day.

This is why the guardrails exist, and why we start with $5.

Three real ways this fails
1
We buy after the news
The domino already fell, so the price already moved. We may pay up for something the crowd figured out first. That is why the bot never pays more than 95¢.
2
One domino is not always a chain
Plenty of small liquidations happen and nothing follows. Sometimes the cascade is already finished by the time we see it.
3
Nobody has tested this on the past
There is no saved history of who was near liquidation last year. So there is no backtest. We are finding out live, with small money, and writing down every single window.
